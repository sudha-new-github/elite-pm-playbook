Leadership philosophy
1
Clarity over comfort
Ambiguity is the enemy of execution. A good leader's first job is to make the path obvious — even when it's hard.
2
Delegate the what, own the why
Leaders who stay in the operational weeds trade executive presence for the illusion of control. The why belongs to you. The how belongs to your team.
3
Narrative is strategy
The best PMs and leaders don't just make decisions — they make decisions legible. A compelling story about where you're going is itself a form of leadership.
4
Play the long game
Short-term wins compound differently than long-term trust. The most important career and product decisions aren't urgent — they're foundational.

"Too many operations, too little executive presence — that's the trap most product leaders fall into without realizing it."

Personal leadership challenge I'm actively working through
Faith & work

I teach adult faith education classes and preach sermons — and it has made me a significantly better product leader. Not despite the tension between the two worlds, but because of it.

Teaching a mixed audience of believers and curious skeptics every week is the best training I know for leading with felt need rather than doctrine. You can't assume shared context. You have to earn attention. You have to make abstract ideas land in real life.

That's exactly what great product work requires. The user doesn't care about your architecture. They care about their problem. The framework is the sermon — the product is the moment it lands.

Faith also gives me a longer time horizon than most. It's hard to panic over a bad quarter when your operating framework runs on something bigger than a fiscal year.

The frameworks

Leverage, Neutral, Overhead

How to protect your highest-value work from noise

Popularized by Shreyas Doshi — ex-Google, Stripe, Twitter

L > N > O
Energy allocation
Leverage (L)

Work that creates disproportionate value. Identify these and go deep — even if it takes 10x the effort of a Neutral task. Elite PMs spend 80% of their energy here.

Neutral (N)

Work that matters but doesn't compound. Do it well enough to be reliable — but don't over-invest. Most of your to-do list lives here.

Overhead (O)

Work that consumes time without creating value. Ruthlessly minimize or eliminate. The trap: overhead often feels like progress because it's busy and visible.

The honest check: Most PMs have inverted ratios — too much O and N, not enough L. The real skill isn't categorizing tasks. It's being brutally honest about which bucket your actual calendar reflects.

Impact × Confidence × Ease

Fast, comparative scoring for a crowded backlog

Used widely across Meta growth teams

I × C × E
ICE score
Impact (1–10)

How much will this move the needle on your north star metric? Not "it'll help users" — "it targets the drop-off at step 3 affecting 40% of signups."

Confidence (1–10)

How sure are you the impact estimate is right? Low confidence = you need more signal before committing resources. Honest scoring here separates good PMs from hopeful ones.

Ease (1–10)

How much effort does this require? Low ease = high cost. Don't penalize hard work unfairly — weight ease against the impact, not on its own.

The output isn't gospel — it's a forcing function for honest conversation. Two people scoring the same item differently is useful data, not a problem to resolve.

Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort

For teams operating at massive user scale

Developed at Intercom, adopted across Google and Meta

R×I×C/E
RICE score
Reach

How many users does this affect per time period? Anchors discussion in real numbers — prevents PMs from inflating impact on low-reach features.

Impact

Per-user impact on the goal. Use a fixed scale: 3 = massive, 2 = high, 1 = medium, 0.5 = low. Consistency across items matters more than precision.

Confidence + Effort

Confidence % discounts the score for uncertainty. Effort in person-weeks is the denominator. Rigorous but requires real data — best for quarterly planning cycles.

At scale (1B+ users), Reach alone filters out half the backlog. Features that don't reach 10M users rarely justify the engineering cost. That filter is the entire point.

Now / Next / Later

Roadmap clarity without false precision

Used by Nikhyl Singhal — Meta, Google Photos

3 buckets
No dates needed
Now

Actively in progress or starting this sprint. Committed, resourced, owned. If something is "Now" it has a DRI and a ship date — no ambiguity.

Next

Directionally decided but not yet started. Enough definition to have a rough size and owner. If it can't move to Now within a quarter, it belongs in Later.

Later

Ideas, bets, hypotheses. No commitment. A Later list with 100 items is a graveyard — it signals poor upstream filtering, not ambitious thinking.

The real value: Stakeholders pushing "urgent" items have to place them in Now, Next, or Later — which forces an honest tradeoff against what's already committed.

Outcome-first prioritization

Start with the metric, work backwards to the work

Core to how Google and Meta set OKRs at team level

Why → What
Not What → Why
Step 1 — name the outcome

What specific, measurable thing needs to be true in 90 days? Not "improve retention" — "increase D30 retention from 22% to 28% for new users who complete onboarding."

Step 2 — map the levers

What are the 3–5 things that most directly move that metric? Only work that connects to a lever gets into the roadmap. Everything else goes to Later automatically.

Step 3 — prioritize by leverage

Among items that move the levers, rank by expected impact per unit of effort. No lever connection = no roadmap entry. No debate needed.

Most teams skip Step 1. They jump straight to features. The discipline is in naming the outcome precisely — that single sentence eliminates 60% of your backlog before you've scored anything.
